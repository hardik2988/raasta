Eight principles, each doing a job

Every principle below is used on a real page of Raasta. For each one: what it is, where it appears, why it was chosen, and how it helps people use the site. Each demo has a button so you can switch the principle off and see what is lost.

Visible in the layout: proximity, similarity, common region, figure-ground, focal point. Shown through interaction: continuity, closure, common fate.

1 · Visible

Proximity

Things placed close together are seen as a group.

Where it is used
Route steps are stacked tightly with the distance beside each one. The A and B fields sit together, away from the filter chips. On the Places directory, cards sit in clear groups with consistent spacing, and in the planner, nearest places cluster right under their category button. On the map, buildings of one block cluster are drawn close together.
Why
A route is one thing, not eight separate lines. Small gaps inside a group and larger gaps between groups say "these belong together" without any extra label.
How it helps
Users find the A/B fields first, then the filters, then the result, in that order, without reading a heading for each part.

2 · Visible

Similarity

Things that look alike are seen as the same kind of thing.

Where it is used
Every map pin has a colour, a shape, and an inner letter identifier (C, H, F, S, M, L, G, K) for its type: blue circles with "C" for academic (classroom) blocks, orange squares with "H" for hostels, pink diamonds with "F" for food and services, green triangles with "S" for sports, purple hexagons with "M" for administration, teal shields with "L" for landmarks such as Gandhi Chowk, deep red arches with "G" for the campus gates, and indigo stars with "K" for children services such as the Kinder Garden. On the Places directory, every card within the same category shares an identical visual structure and category badge. All primary buttons share one style.
Why
The campus has 21 places. Reading 21 labels is slow; scanning for a colour, shape, or letter is fast. Using shape and an inner letter as well as colour means people who cannot tell colours apart can still read the map immediately.
How it helps
Someone looking for "any hostel" can spot the orange squares with "H" in a second. Buttons that look the same teach users what is clickable.

3 · Visible

Common region

Things inside the same boundary are seen as a group.

Where it is used
The planner, the map and each persona on the home page sit in their own white cards. On the map, soft dashed outlines mark the Academic core, the Hostel zone and Blocks 10 and 11.
Why
A boundary groups things more strongly than colour or spacing, so it is the right tool to separate the planner from the map and to name campus zones.
How it helps
Users know which controls belong to the planner and which belong to the map. The zones tell a newcomer that "Blocks 1 to 8" are one area.

4 · Visible

Figure-ground

We separate a focus object (the figure) from its background (the ground).

Where it is used
When a route is chosen, the map fades back and the route comes forward in bright coral with a white outline. The two chosen places grow larger and get an A or B badge, and every other pin steps back. When tapping any place, the place detail modal panel emerges as the prominent figure while the entire background dims under a semi-transparent backdrop.
Why
A campus map has roads, buildings and labels everywhere. Only one path matters at a given moment, so the rest must become background.
How it helps
The user never has to hunt for the route in a busy picture. It is the first thing the eye lands on.

Live demo · Library to Food Court

Dimmed, the route is the figure. At full strength, the map competes with it.

5 · Visible

Focal point

The element that stands out most is seen first.

Where it is used
The home page has one large filled "Find a route" button and one quiet outlined button. On the route finder, the coral route and the pulsing B marker are the strongest elements on screen.
Why
Most visitors have a single goal: get a route. One loud action avoids choice paralysis and makes the first step obvious.
How it helps
A new user needs no instructions. The eye goes to the biggest, darkest button, and that is the right one.

Live demo · home page actions

When all three buttons look equal, the main action has to be found by reading.

6 · Interaction

Continuity

The eye follows a smooth line or path and expects it to keep going.

Where it is used
The route is one unbroken line that draws itself from A to B, with small moving dashes showing the direction. The three-step progress bar on the route finder (Where are you, Where to, Walk) fills in as a line. The dotted rail beside the steps connects them in order.
Why
Wayfinding is about direction. A continuous line tells people where to go next more clearly than a list of turns.
How it helps
Users can follow the path with their eyes before reading a single step, and they can see how far through the task they are.

Live demo · Library to Block 11

The line draws itself from start to finish, and the eye follows it.

7 · Interaction

Closure

The mind completes shapes that have small gaps.

Where it is used
The Raasta logo is a ring with a gap around a single dot. The progress ring in the route result fills as you tick off steps and closes when you arrive. The campus zones are dashed outlines that the eye reads as complete shapes.
Why
An unfinished ring asks to be finished. That makes "arrive" feel like a goal rather than a button, and it keeps the zone outlines light.
How it helps
The ring shows progress at a glance, and the dashed zones group places without hiding the map.

8 · Interaction

Common fate

Things that move together are seen as belonging together.

Where it is used
On the route finder, choose "Hostels" in the filter and every hostel pin on the map bobs up and down together while the rest fade. The same happens for academic blocks, food, sports and administration.
Why
Shared motion is a stronger grouping signal than colour alone, and it works even when pins are far apart on the map.
How it helps
A user can answer "where are all the food places?" at a glance, without reading labels.
